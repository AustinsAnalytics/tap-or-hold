Tap or Hold

Every ABS challenge is a bet. Tap, and you might flip a call that swings the game. Lose, and you've spent the one thing that could save you later. I priced both sides of that bet for all 371,623 called pitches of 2026 and turned the answer into a dugout card.

The short version

Six findings, four recommendations

  1. I rebuilt the robot umpire from public data. It reproduces 7,888 of 7,891 real 2026 challenges (99.96%), so every missed call below is measured, not guessed.
  2. A challenge has a price, and it moves. Baseball Savant's public guideline charges a flat 0.20 runs for losing one. Replaying the season, a club's last challenge is worth about 0.13 runs in the 1st inning, 0.05 by the 7th and 0.01 in the 9th. A spare (one of two) is worth about 57% as much.
  3. Players don't price the moment. On clear misses, players tapped 24% of the cheapest calls and 36% of calls worth 94× more. Priced correctly, those numbers should be 13% and 85%.
  4. Tap more, win less, gain more. The card taps 2.8 times a game (clubs: 1.6) and wins only 40% of them (clubs: 56%), yet recovers 3.5 wins a season to the clubs' 2.1.
  5. Nobody adjusts when a lineup is tapped out. By the 9th, 11% of pitches go to a lineup with no challenges left. Umpires don't call it differently and pitchers don't attack the umpire's extra strikes any more often.
  6. Altitude raises the stakes, not the errors. At Coors the same call is worth 8% more and four-seamers lose 3.6 in. of ride, but umpires miss at the same rate and visitors challenge just as accurately.
What I'd tell the Rockies' staff
  • Price every tap. Hold early unless you're sure; from the 7th on in a close game, a coin-flip call is worth a tap; never finish a close game with a challenge unused. The card gives the exact bar for every count, inning and score.
  • Give the defensive trigger to the catcher. Catchers won 55% of 4,292 taps in 2026; pitchers won 40% of 133. Hunter Goodman won 53 of 81 (65%).
  • Hitters: be stingy early, bold with two strikes. Rockies hitters won 49% of their taps (league 58%). The value is concentrated: the top 10% of missed calls carry 37% of all the value.
  • Watch the other dugout's count. When a lineup is out of challenges, the umpire's zone is final. Pitch to it (see the map).

Rockies 2026: 8.7 wins of missed calls went against them; they won back 2.2. The card would have won back 3.2.

The price of a tap

When is a challenge worth spending?

A call goes against you. You believe, with probability p, that ABS would overturn it. Tapping wins V (the win probability the flipped call is worth) with probability p, and costs D (what the challenge is worth for the rest of the game) with probability 1 − p, because a successful challenge is kept. So tap when

tap if  p  ≥  DV + D

V is easy: a win-probability model that matches Savant's published values (correlation 0.99986) prices every call in every state. D is the hard part, and it is what Savant fixes at 0.20 runs. Like an option, a challenge has time value: it is worth what you can do with it later, so it decays as the game runs out, and the last one is worth more than a spare. I estimated D by replaying all 2,429 games: each club's real remaining calls, with their real values and real umpire misses, played forward with and without one more challenge, using simulated eyesight fitted to how 2026 players actually challenged (details in Method).

The last challenge is worth 1.29% of a win in the 1st inning of a tie game, 0.99% in the 7th and 0.46% in the 9th. In runs, that is 0.13 → 0.05 → 0.01: below the flat guideline everywhere, and far below it late. That one gap drives most of what follows: the flat rule makes players too stingy, most of all when it matters most.

What a better policy is worth

Replaying 2026 with the card

Every policy below faces the same 255,378 calls with the same simulated eyesight (24 draws per call). The card's price table was always estimated on one half of the games and scored on the other half, so no policy is graded on games it learned from.

The card recovers 3.5 wins per club per season, against 2.1 for what clubs actually did and 2.0 for the flat 0.20-run rule. It gets there by tapping more (2.8 taps a game against 1.6) and losing more (40% success against 56%): most of the time a lost challenge is cheap, and one left unused at the final out is worth nothing.

Range across clubs for the card: 2.8–4.2 wins. With a more pessimistic eyesight model the card still recovers 3.2 wins (+1.2 over actual; flat rule 1.6). Fixing every missed call would be worth 9.6.

Players and the zone

Eyes, will and stakes

Clear misses challenged, by what the call was worth
8,368 calls missed by 1–4 in. with a challenge in hand, grouped into tenths by value.

The most valuable tenth of clear misses was worth 94 times the cheapest tenth. Players barely noticed: they tapped 24% of the cheap ones and 36% of the expensive ones. Priced correctly, the gap is 13% to 85%.

To simulate realistic decisions I fitted a model of every 2026 challenge opportunity in which a player sees how wrong the call was with some error (his eyesight) and, if what he sees clears his bar, taps with some probability (his willingness). Catchers and pitchers read the zone to within about 1.9 in. (95% CI 1.8–2.0); hitters to within about 2.6 in. (2.4–2.8). Even when the call looks clearly wrong, hitters tap only 68% of the time and batteries 77%. A tenfold jump in the stakes moves a hitter's bar by just 0.42 in.

Who should tap? On defense, catchers won 55% of 4,292 challenges and pitchers 40% of 133; hitters won 58% of 3,466.

Tapped out

When the robot zone disappears

Once a lineup has no challenges left, the umpire's call is final, and the umpire's zone is not the ABS zone. By the 7th inning 5.7% of pitches go to a tapped-out lineup; by the 8th 8.2%; by the 9th 11%.

Nobody adjusts. Umpires call strikes outside the ABS zone at the same rate relative to location (0.94 of expected when the lineup is out, 0.92 when it isn't). And pitchers don't throw to the umpire's extra strike area any more often: it takes 1.6% of pitches either way (change with the lineup out: 0.00 points, standard error 0.08, with pitcher, count and inning controls).

That is a free edge. When the opponent is tapped out, a borderline pitch in the orange areas of the map is a called strike that can no longer be taken back.

Coors Field

Altitude raises the stakes, not the errors

At 5,200 feet the ball moves less: 2026 four-seamers at Coors averaged 12.1 in. of induced vertical break against 15.7 elsewhere, and 6.2 in. of horizontal break against 8.0. Half-innings at Coors produced 0.62 runs against 0.49 (27% more).

So the win-probability model has a Coors version: the same run distributions tilted by one parameter fitted to every Coors half-inning. It forecasts Coors games better than the league version (Brier 0.1662 vs 0.1669). In the same situation a flipped call at Coors is worth 8% more, and the card's bar at Coors sits 1.2 points lower in close games.

What altitude does not change: umpires missed 11.3% of called strikes at Coors and 10.9% elsewhere (4.0% and 4.1% of called balls), and visiting players won 57% of 117 challenges at Coors (95% CI 48%–66%) against 56% everywhere else. A saved challenge is worth about the same at Coors too (multiplier 0.90, 95% CI 0.73–1.09), so the card keeps one price table and lets each call's stakes do the work.

The Rockies' 2026

An elite battery, hitters who leave value behind

Missed calls cost the Rockies 8.7 wins in 2026; they won back 2.2 (13th in MLB). On defense they ranked 5th in challenge success: 92 of 151 won (61%, league 55%), led by Hunter Goodman at 53 of 81. At the plate they ranked 30th: 66 of 135 (49%, league 58%).

The costliest one was the one they couldn't challenge. On April 10 at SD, tied in the bottom of the 9th, a full-count pitch to Manny Machado 2.5 in. inside the ABS zone was called ball four. The Rockies were already out of challenges. That call was worth 18% of a win.

The hitters' misses had a second cost: failed taps run the lineup dry. The Rockies were out of challenges for 12% of the pitches they saw from the 7th inning on, 5th most in MLB (league average 8%), so late in games their hitters faced the umpire's zone more than anyone's.

Replaying their season with the card: 3.2 wins won back instead of 2.2.

How it works

Method and checks

  1. Data. Every 2026 regular-season pitch from Baseball Savant (716,792) joined to the MLB Stats API's pitch-by-pitch review records, which flag each ABS challenge, who made it and whether it was overturned (7,891 challenges, all matched). Loaded into DuckDB; cleaning and joins are written in SQL.
  2. The ABS zone. A strike if any part of the ball touches the batter's rectangle: 17 in. wide, from 27% to 53.5% of his certified height. Savant's published zone tops and bottoms match those percentages exactly. I tested where to measure the pitch and how big the ball is against the 7,891 known verdicts: the published plate coordinates with a 1.45-in. radius reproduce 99.96%; moving the pitch to mid-plate, as the rule's wording suggests, reproduces at best 85.9%, and ignoring the ball's size 68.5%.
  3. Win probability. For any state, the rest of the half-inning comes from the empirical distribution of runs scored from that base-out-count state, and each later half-inning from that inning's run distribution; convolving them gives the final margin. It matches Savant's published win expectancy (correlation 0.99986, mean gap 0.37%) and predicts final results as well (Brier 0.1576 vs 0.1577). The Coors version tilts every distribution by one fitted parameter (θ = 0.104). Run expectancy by count matches Savant's (correlation 0.995).
  4. Eyesight and willingness. P(tap) = willingness × Φ((miss − bar) / eyesight), fitted by maximum likelihood to every challenge opportunity, separately for hitters and batteries; the bar may move with the stakes, the inning and the challenges left. It beats a plain probit on AIC by 7.0 (hitters) and 17.9 (batteries). Calibrated beliefs come from Bayes' rule with the 2026 distribution of misses as the prior.
  5. The price of a challenge. A season replay: for every club-game and every point in it, the club's real remaining calls are played forward with 0, 1 and 2 challenges under the card's own thresholds, 24 eyesight draws per call, including the extra-inning rule. The difference is D. Three rounds of policy iteration; challenge values are averaged by inning, home/away, score and challenges left.
  6. Scoring policies. Two-fold cross-validation by game; every policy sees the same calls and the same eyesight draws. Sensitivity: a more pessimistic eyesight model (plain probit: 2.9 in. hitters, 2.1 in. batteries).

Limits

The replay holds the rest of each real game fixed after a simulated flip; a flipped call would have changed what came next, so the values are first-order. Simulated players use calibrated beliefs; real ones are not always calibrated. Leaving a challenge unused costs nothing in the model, but taps have real costs the data can't see: time, an umpire's patience, a hitter's focus. The Coors sample is one season of 81 games. Public tracking may differ slightly from what ABS measures, though 3 of 7,891 verdicts disagree.
