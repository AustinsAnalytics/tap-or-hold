The short version

Six findings, four recommendations

  1. I rebuilt the robot umpire from public data. It reproduces {{zone_matched}} of {{zone_challenges}} real 2026 challenges ({{zone_agree}}), so every missed call below is measured, not guessed.
  2. A challenge has a price, and it moves. Baseball Savant's public guideline charges a flat 0.20 runs for losing one. Replaying the season, a club's last challenge is worth about {{price_k1_inn1_runs}} runs in the 1st inning, {{price_k1_inn7_runs}} by the 7th and {{price_k1_inn9_runs}} in the 9th. A spare (one of two) is worth about {{k2_share_of_k1}} as much.
  3. Players don't price the moment. On clear misses, players tapped {{stakes_tap_low}} of the cheapest calls and {{stakes_tap_high}} of calls worth {{stakes_ratio}}× more. Priced correctly, those numbers should be {{stakes_card_low}} and {{stakes_card_high}}.
  4. Tap more, win less, gain more. The card taps {{card_taps}} times a game (clubs: {{actual_taps}}) and wins only {{card_winrate}} of them (clubs: {{actual_winrate}}), yet recovers {{card_wins}} wins a season to the clubs' {{actual_wins}}.
  5. Nobody adjusts when a lineup is tapped out. By the 9th, {{tapped_9th}} of pitches go to a lineup with no challenges left. Umpires don't call it differently and pitchers don't attack the umpire's extra strikes any more often.
  6. Altitude raises the stakes, not the errors. At Coors the same call is worth {{coors_v_lift}} more and four-seamers lose {{ivb_drop}} in. of ride, but umpires miss at the same rate and visitors challenge just as accurately.
What I'd tell the Rockies' staff
  • Price every tap. Hold early unless you're sure; from the 7th on in a close game, a coin-flip call is worth a tap; never finish a close game with a challenge unused. The card gives the exact bar for every count, inning and score.
  • Give the defensive trigger to the catcher. Catchers won {{catcher_rate}} of {{catcher_taps}} taps in 2026; pitchers won {{pitcher_rate}} of {{pitcher_taps}}. Hunter Goodman won {{goodman_ot}} of {{goodman_taps}} ({{goodman_rate}}).
  • Hitters: be stingy early, bold with two strikes. Rockies hitters won {{col_off_rate}} of their taps (league {{lg_off_rate}}). The value is concentrated: the top 10% of missed calls carry {{top10_share}} of all the value.
  • Watch the other dugout's count. When a lineup is out of challenges, the umpire's zone is final. Pitch to it (see the map).

The price of a tap

When is a challenge worth spending?

A call goes against you. You believe, with probability p, that ABS would overturn it. Tapping wins V (the win probability the flipped call is worth) with probability p, and costs D (what the challenge is worth for the rest of the game) with probability 1 − p, because a successful challenge is kept. So tap when

tap if  p  ≥  DV + D

V is easy: a win-probability model that matches Savant's published values (correlation {{we_corr}}) prices every call in every state. D is the hard part, and it is what Savant fixes at 0.20 runs. Like an option, a challenge has time value: it is worth what you can do with it later, so it decays as the game runs out, and the last one is worth more than a spare. I estimated D by replaying all {{games}} games: each club's real remaining calls, with their real values and real umpire misses, played forward with and without one more challenge, using simulated eyesight fitted to how 2026 players actually challenged (details in Method).

Tapped out

When the robot zone disappears

Once a lineup has no challenges left, the umpire's call is final, and the umpire's zone is not the ABS zone. By the 7th inning {{tapped_7th}} of pitches go to a tapped-out lineup; by the 8th {{tapped_8th}}; by the 9th {{tapped_9th}}.

Nobody adjusts. Umpires call strikes outside the ABS zone at the same rate relative to location ({{ump_out_ratio}} of expected when the lineup is out, {{ump_has_ratio}} when it isn't). And pitchers don't throw to the umpire's extra strike area any more often: it takes {{gift_share}} of pitches either way (change with the lineup out: {{gift_coef}} points, standard error {{gift_se}}, with pitcher, count and inning controls).

That is a free edge. When the opponent is tapped out, a borderline pitch in the orange areas of the map is a called strike that can no longer be taken back.

Coors Field

Altitude raises the stakes, not the errors

At 5,200 feet the ball moves less: 2026 four-seamers at Coors averaged {{ivb_coors}} in. of induced vertical break against {{ivb_else}} elsewhere, and {{hb_coors}} in. of horizontal break against {{hb_else}}. Half-innings at Coors produced {{coors_runs_half}} runs against {{else_runs_half}} ({{coors_runs_lift}} more).

So the win-probability model has a Coors version: the same run distributions tilted by one parameter fitted to every Coors half-inning. It forecasts Coors games better than the league version (Brier {{brier_coors_coors}} vs {{brier_coors_league}}). In the same situation a flipped call at Coors is worth {{coors_v_lift}} more, and the card's bar at Coors sits {{coors_pstar_drop}} points lower in close games.

What altitude does not change: umpires missed {{coors_miss_strikes}} of called strikes at Coors and {{else_miss_strikes}} elsewhere ({{coors_miss_balls}} and {{else_miss_balls}} of called balls), and visiting players won {{vis_coors_rate}} of {{vis_coors_n}} challenges at Coors (95% CI {{vis_coors_lo}}–{{vis_coors_hi}}) against {{else_rate}} everywhere else. A saved challenge is worth about the same at Coors too (multiplier {{kappa1}}, 95% CI {{kappa1_lo}}–{{kappa1_hi}}), so the card keeps one price table and lets each call's stakes do the work.

How it works

Method and checks

  1. Data. Every 2026 regular-season pitch from Baseball Savant ({{pitches}}) joined to the MLB Stats API's pitch-by-pitch review records, which flag each ABS challenge, who made it and whether it was overturned ({{challenges}} challenges, all matched). Loaded into DuckDB; cleaning and joins are written in SQL.
  2. The ABS zone. A strike if any part of the ball touches the batter's rectangle: 17 in. wide, from 27% to 53.5% of his certified height. Savant's published zone tops and bottoms match those percentages exactly. I tested where to measure the pitch and how big the ball is against the {{zone_challenges}} known verdicts: the published plate coordinates with a {{ball_radius}}-in. radius reproduce {{zone_agree}}; moving the pitch to mid-plate, as the rule's wording suggests, reproduces at best {{zone_mid_best}}, and ignoring the ball's size {{zone_no_radius}}.
  3. Win probability. For any state, the rest of the half-inning comes from the empirical distribution of runs scored from that base-out-count state, and each later half-inning from that inning's run distribution; convolving them gives the final margin. It matches Savant's published win expectancy (correlation {{we_corr}}, mean gap {{we_mad}}) and predicts final results as well (Brier {{brier_ours}} vs {{brier_savant}}). The Coors version tilts every distribution by one fitted parameter (θ = {{coors_theta}}). Run expectancy by count matches Savant's (correlation {{re_count_corr}}).
  4. Eyesight and willingness. P(tap) = willingness × Φ((miss − bar) / eyesight), fitted by maximum likelihood to every challenge opportunity, separately for hitters and batteries; the bar may move with the stakes, the inning and the challenges left. It beats a plain probit on AIC by {{off_aic_gain}} (hitters) and {{def_aic_gain}} (batteries). Calibrated beliefs come from Bayes' rule with the 2026 distribution of misses as the prior.
  5. The price of a challenge. A season replay: for every club-game and every point in it, the club's real remaining calls are played forward with 0, 1 and 2 challenges under the card's own thresholds, {{sim_draws}} eyesight draws per call, including the extra-inning rule. The difference is D. Three rounds of policy iteration; challenge values are averaged by inning, home/away, score and challenges left.
  6. Scoring policies. Two-fold cross-validation by game; every policy sees the same calls and the same eyesight draws. Sensitivity: a more pessimistic eyesight model (plain probit: {{off_probit_sigma}} in. hitters, {{def_probit_sigma}} in. batteries).

Limits

The replay holds the rest of each real game fixed after a simulated flip; a flipped call would have changed what came next, so the values are first-order. Simulated players use calibrated beliefs; real ones are not always calibrated. Leaving a challenge unused costs nothing in the model, but taps have real costs the data can't see: time, an umpire's patience, a hitter's focus. The Coors sample is one season of 81 games. Public tracking may differ slightly from what ABS measures, though {{zone_mismatches}} of {{zone_challenges}} verdicts disagree.
